Inside the experiment

Shells of tetrahedra

Each dango is an icosphere skin, an inner shell and a centre point, joined into prisms and pyramids and split into about 1,300 tetrahedra. Every tet is pulled toward its best-fit rotation of its rest shape and keeps its volume — mochi is nearly incompressible — so a squeezed ball bulges and a stretched one thins.

Memory

Mochi is viscoplastic. Past a small yield, each tet's rest shape slowly creeps toward the shape it's being held in, so a poke leaves a dimple and a slow pull leaves a longer, thinner ball. Roll them round eases every rest shape back to the sphere.

Tearing

A tet stretched far past its rest shape breaks. Its nodes are split wherever the surrounding tets no longer share a face, so a crack opens, and the new surface is found again from the faces that belong to one tet only. Slow pulls string out; fast ones snap.

Sticking

Where two pieces touch, little bonds form between their particles. They hold a pressed seam together and stretch before they let go — stronger as stickiness rises.

The skewer

The bamboo is a rigid rod resting in a ceramic chopstick rest. Each ball is threaded on it by its centre and poles, which slide along the stick with friction; pull a ball past the tip and it comes off.

Round, without the polygons

The tets are coarse, so the surface is rebuilt on the GPU: every boundary triangle becomes a curved PN patch from its corner positions and normals. The original skin is dusted with starch; torn flesh is smooth and a little glossy, and thin strands glow where light comes through.
